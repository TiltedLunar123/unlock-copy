The quiet economics of the municipal water tower

By R. Ostrander · 11 min read

Every town of a certain size owns one, and almost nobody who lives under it can say what it cost. The tower on the hill above Milford holds 640,000 gallons, went up in 1974, and has been repainted four times since, most recently in a shade the council minutes describe only as "approved gray."

The pressure in your kitchen tap is a decision somebody made in a meeting, and it is almost always the same decision: put the tank where gravity is free.

Water towers are a bet on elevation. Lift the water once, overnight, when power is cheap, then let the weight of it do the distribution all day for nothing. A tank sitting 120 feet up delivers roughly 52 pounds per square inch at the ground, which is why the height of the tower and the pressure at your sink are the same fact written two ways.

The maintenance schedule is where the real money hides. Interior coatings last twenty to thirty years, and draining a tower to recoat it means running the system on pumps alone for a season, which is the expensive way to do what gravity was doing free.
